# Bullseye Strategy → One-Page Endgame

A strategy is a theory of how you win — and mostly a list of what you won't do. Without one, energy scatters in ten directions and nothing moves far.

Start with a self-rating: how obvious a choice are you in your market, 1–10?

## Step 1 — Obvious Choice statement
Which market can you dominate? Narrow until it's believable: not "CRM for SMBs" but "CRM for real-estate agents".
Template: *We are the obvious choice for [specific segment] who struggle with [specific problem].*
Think big but confident; update over time.

## Step 2 — Where to play
Fill in, specifically: ideal customer (the group that loves the product as it is today) · main problem solved · core product (drives most revenue) · primary marketing channels (1–2) · geography.

## Step 3 — How to win: moats
Pick 1–2 to build now, aim for ~3 over time (one can be copied; three together rarely). Moats need maintenance.
1. Differentiation — distinct value, niche, quality
2. Cost leadership — lowest cost at acceptable quality
3. User experience — seamless across the whole journey
4. Network effects — more users = more value (templates, marketplaces)
5. Operational efficiency — leaner processes, faster delivery
6. Brand & reputation — trust, premium pricing
7. Distribution — exclusive channels, partnerships, early app-store/SEO position
8. Speed & innovation — the fast eat the slow
9. Pricing — model aligned with customer success (per transaction, no per-seat fees)
10. Free — free tools that feed the core product
11. Switching cost — integrations, data, workflow lock-in
12. Proprietary — IP, patents, a named methodology
13. Engagement — community, user-generated content
14. Expansion — highest lifetime value (land and expand across products)
15. Founder brand — a visible, trusted founder
(Customer support can also be a moat when users are non-technical.)
Common combos: low-cost leader = cost + operational efficiency · go upmarket = differentiation + experience + switching cost · dominate = cost + efficiency + UX · disrupt a stagnant industry = free + speed + cost.
Write each moat with its reason.

## Step 4 — Winning picture
- **Endgame**, SMART, 3–10 years out (exit, IPO, profit, impact, customers served). Not "leave a legacy".
- **1-Year Picture**: date · top outcomes (revenue, revenue per employee, profit) · 3 SMART top goals · optional team reward.
- **Quarterly Picture**: same fields, must support the year.
- **Monthly Picture**: top outcomes · ONE top focus · top 3 projects · reward.

## Step 5 — Strategic choices
- **3 core values** (hire/fire by them; tip: the opposite of what annoys you about other companies).
- **Core capabilities**: 2–3 per moat. Which to build new, which to double down on, which are no longer needed.
- **Supplemental choices** = explicit "no"s derived from where-to-play (e.g. stop signing enterprise custom deals that eat 90% of dev time), how-to-win, and the 1-year picture. A tactic ("test Meta ads") isn't a strategic choice; "Meta is our one channel, no others this year" is.

## Output: One-Page Endgame (OPE)
Obvious choice · where to play table · moats + reasons · endgame · 1-year / quarter / month pictures · values · capabilities · strategic no's.
Share with everyone who acts on it, is affected by it, or can give feedback. Review it quarterly (see `plg-growth-process`). If you're 100% confident, you waited too long.

Source: *The Product-Led Playbook* (Wes Bush), ch. 1 (adapted from Lafley & Martin's *Playing to Win*). Unofficial summary.
